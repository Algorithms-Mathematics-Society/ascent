The website's first job is unusual, and it follows from Ascent being an unusual contest: most CP contest sites just list problems, dates, and prizes because everyone already knows what an ICPC-style contest is. Ascent isn't that — it's scored on  *performance* , not just correctness, and it ends with "optimize a real codebase." So the single biggest thing the site has to do is make a non-standard format **legible and desirable** at a glance, because if a strong competitor can't tell what Ascent is or how to prepare for it, they won't register, and if a sponsor can't tell what's different, the site does nothing for your pitch. Clarity of the format *is* the marketing here.

On what to keep in mind while building it:

The format explanation has to carry more weight than usual. Spell out the three-round arc (C++ qualifier → team optimization at the IIT hubs → the codebase finale at IITB) and, crucially, explain *what you're scored on* — that performance against a baseline matters, not just a green checkmark. People prepare for what they think is being measured, so if you don't say "this is an optimization contest, judged on speedup," they'll prep like it's Codeforces and arrive mismatched.

Publish the environment and rules spec, or at least signal it's coming. For an optimization contest the C++ standard, compiler, flags, target hardware, and which libraries/intrinsics/threading are allowed materially change both results and preparation — serious systems people will look for exactly this, and its presence signals you run a rigorous contest rather than a vibes-based one. Vagueness here reads as amateur to precisely the audience you most want.

Address integrity and the online/offline split honestly and upfront. You're running an unproctored-online pool against self-centre offline IITians feeding one ranklist, and C++-only implementation problems are exactly what an LLM one-shots. The site should state the proctoring approach and how qualification works (separate pools/quotas, or independent cutoffs) so the format reads as fair — this matters more for credibility than for a quant-reasoning contest like Derive.

Make eligibility and the India-vs-APAC scope unambiguous, since this is your first regional edition with limited seats outside India — confusion there costs you registrations from exactly the international talent that makes Ascent bigger than Derive. And fold the consent layer into registration (the DPDP opt-ins we built), so the top of the funnel captures the talent-graph data cleanly from day one rather than bolting it on later.

Lean on Derive for credibility, accurately. The 2.5K registrations, the IITB finale, and your sponsor logos are legitimate trust signals and belong on the site (Derive's site already shows Jane Street publicly, so this is consistent). But be careful with superlatives — "first big C++ contest in India" is the kind of claim a knowledgeable visitor or a sponsor can dispute (Citadel's Terminal and others exist in the algo-contest space), so lead with the *substance* of what's different rather than a "first" you'd have to defend. Visually, keep continuity with the AMS quant-terminal look but give Ascent its own summit/gradient identity so it reads as a distinct property, not a recolored Derive.

On the unique things the site should actually convey — this is where Ascent can stand apart from every other contest page:

**"This is the actual job."** The optimization-codebase finale mirrors real low-latency / HFT engineering more closely than any puzzle does. Make that the hero message — Ascent isn't abstract problem-solving, it's the work itself. That's simultaneously the differentiator for competitors and the exact thing that makes Optiver and Citadel care.

**Show the optimization, don't just describe it.** The strongest unique element you can put on the page is a concrete  *taste* : a slow C++ snippet next to its optimized version with a real speedup number, or a small "before/after" with a performance delta. Letting a visitor *see* what "optimize this" looks like conveys the contest's identity instantly in a way no paragraph can, and it's something no standard CP site has.

**Make performance the visual motif.** Since the contest is scored on speed, lean into it — speedup badges ("3.2× faster"), a performance/latency aesthetic, the "climb the gradient" double meaning (gradient *ascent* as both the optimization term and the summit metaphor). A contour/loss-landscape-as-mountain visual carries optimization + ascent + "climb the ranklist" in one image, which is on-brand for AMS and unique to this event.

**Be transparent about how you measure.** Publishing your evaluation methodology — speedup vs baseline, best-of-N runs on fixed hardware, correctness preserved or you score zero — is unusual and builds real trust with a serious audience, and it differentiates you from contests that hand-wave scoring.

**Convey the recruiting payoff.** For this audience, the real prize isn't cash, it's being seen by top firms — finalists getting in front of the people who actually hire for this work. Conveying "the best firms watch this contest" is both the strongest draw and entirely on-strategy, since the funnel-to-recruiting pipeline is the point. You can convey this through your partner presence without over-naming anyone.

The thing tying it together: Derive's identity is *probabilistic reasoning* (the quant-research flavor); Ascent's identity should be *systems performance* (the engineering flavor). The site should feel like it belongs to people who care about microseconds and cache misses — serious, measured, for the best — and the climb metaphor gives you a natural narrative from qualifier to summit.
